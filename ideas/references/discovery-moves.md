# Discovery moves

Use these as choices, not a sequence or a menu to dump on the user. Anchor a move in something the user actually said. Pick one, see what it produces, and adapt.

| When this is happening | Useful move | Example question shape |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Interests are broad or abstract | Zoom into a recent episode | "What was the last specific moment that caught your attention?" |
| A recurring annoyance appears | Trace the friction before proposing a fix | "Where did it first become harder than you expected?" |
| Two interests share a possible mechanism | Bridge through the shared mechanism | "You described feedback in both activities. Where does that comparison break?" |
| An existing approach is taken for granted | Surface an assumption | "What has to be true for this approach to make sense?" |
| The user knows a field well | Look for exceptions or local workarounds | "When do people who know this well still improvise?" |
| The user has little domain knowledge | Give a compact explanation or observable example | "Given that mechanism, what outcome would you expect?" |
| Goals and available resources conflict | Explore a smaller or different expression of the goal | "What is the smallest version that would still interest you?" |
| An idea is attractive but vague | Find an informative observation | "What could you see or try that would change your mind about it?" |
| The conversation circles | Change the source of input | Invite a brief observation, conversation, sketch, or comparison outside the current discussion. |

## Use productive surprise

An unexpected comparison, exception, artifact, or different perspective can widen the search. Make its relevance understandable without announcing a conclusion. For example, if a user mentions both organizing community events and debugging software, invite them to compare how each makes a hidden problem visible. Do not jump straight to a predetermined app idea.

Stay within reach of their knowledge. If the connection needs unfamiliar material, explain enough for them to reason with it. They should be able to accept, reject, or reshape it without guessing what you want to hear.

If a comparison is interesting only to you, move on. A connection is a hypothesis, not a discovery you need to defend.

## When questions stop helping

- With short or uncertain replies, narrow to something they encountered recently or offer one concrete example they can react to.
- With many interests, compare what draws them to two examples instead of making them pick an identity.
- With excitement but weak evidence, preserve the energy and identify one assumption to explore.
- With discouragement, reduce the size of the next experience. Do not respond with grand claims about their potential.
- With a request for a direct answer, answer it. Explain which parts are your suggestions and return control to the user.
- With conflicting goals, reflect the tension and explore the tradeoff. Do not silently select the goal you favor.
- With an interest that changed, refresh the picture rather than trying to enforce consistency.

## Judge progress by the user

Useful signs include a new connection expressed in their own words, a sharper question, a changed assumption, or an experience they want to try. Producing many ideas, prolonging the conversation, and flattering the user are not measures of progress.
